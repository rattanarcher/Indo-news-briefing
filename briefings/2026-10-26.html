What Happened Last Week

Students and civil society groups protested across Indonesia on October 20 to mark two years of the Prabowo-Gibran administration.

Demonstrations took place in Jakarta, Bandung, Yogyakarta, and Makassar as activists criticized government policies on forest fires, rising living costs, and corruption. BEM UI led protests at Tugu Tani, while Greenpeace activists scaled the Welcome Monument at Hotel Indonesia roundabout to unfurl banners declaring "Haze & Prabowo's Regime = National Disaster." The Yogyakarta protest ended in chaos late Tuesday evening, and protesters in Bandung called for action on forest fires and corruption. The following day, President Prabowo defended his administration's record, saying Indonesians should be proud of achievements made despite strengths and weaknesses, while Amnesty International issued a failing grade on human rights for the two-year period.

President Prabowo's 26-member advisory council faced a Constitutional Court challenge over claims the bloated structure burdens the state budget.

A petitioner challenged Article 7(1) of the 2024 Wantimpres Law, which allows unlimited membership, arguing the 26 advisors combined with 10 special presidential advisors create a total of 36 people advising the president. The lawsuit argues this creates unmeasurable burdens on the state budget for salaries and facilities, with the nine-member limit having been removed just a month before Prabowo took office. Wantimpres member Hatta Rajasa defended the structure, saying they do not view it from the angle of efficiency but rather future benefits aligned with the state's large responsibilities. When asked for comment, Wantimpres chairman Pratikno and member Anwar Usman said they would await the Constitutional Court's discussion of the matter.

Indonesia signed the Badr Memorandum with Turkey, Qatar, and Pakistan on October 21 to boost joint defense production and reduce reliance on foreign suppliers.

The four-nation defense pact was signed in Doha to strengthen cooperation in joint research and development, technology transfer, and industrial manufacturing collaboration. Indonesia's Foreign Ministry said the partnership allows Jakarta to collaborate with nations possessing high qualifications and compatibility to advance domestic defense output. The agreement aims to foster cooperation in defense manufacturing and enhance capabilities among the four countries through joint production and technology sharing. Turkey's Defense Ministry described it as reflecting the countries' desire to deepen cooperation beyond off-the-shelf procurement models that foster sustainable defense industries. The pact drew attention in India, with reports suggesting the move raised concerns about BrahMos missile cooperation given Indonesia's partnership with Pakistan.

Expert Commentary This Week

Writing in The Diplomat, Larry Luckey argues that Indonesia's October 2026 stock market crash, which saw the Jakarta Composite Index plunge 11.6 percent in one day following MSCI's threat to downgrade the country from Emerging Market status, reveals fundamental problems with the credibility of Indonesia's capital markets and regulatory institutions. The crisis stems from coordinated trading schemes where groups of investors manipulate share prices through multiple brokerage accounts, creating artificial demand that misleads foreign institutions and index funds into purchasing overvalued shares. Luckey contends that MSCI's concerns about concentrated ownership, questionable free-float calculations, and market manipulation are not new, having been flagged since January 2026, raising serious questions about the effectiveness of Indonesia's Financial Services Authority.

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Bhima Yudhistira Adhinegara argues that President Prabowo's economic policies have created widespread public stress, with 75 percent of Indonesians feeling anxious about government policies two years into his administration. He contends that Prabowo's 8 percent GDP growth target is dangerously unrealistic and that official statistics no longer reflect actual economic conditions, citing discrepancies between reported manufacturing growth and actual contraction indicators like mass layoffs. Adhinegara further argues that populist programs like the Free Nutritious Meals initiative have triggered price hikes on basic necessities, creating a vicious cycle that has left ordinary households struggling despite official growth figures exceeding 5 percent.
