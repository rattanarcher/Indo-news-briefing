What Happened Last Week

Coalition parties agreed to raise the parliamentary threshold to 5 percent, but the proposal split allies and triggered opposition from smaller parties.

Gerindra Secretary-General Sugiono confirmed on September 16 that coalition party leaders had agreed on a 5 percent threshold for the 2029 election, up from the current 4 percent. Golkar and PKS backed the plan, but PAN initially objected before reversing its stance on September 18 at its anniversary celebration. NasDem's Surya Paloh broke with the coalition on September 19, insisting his party would propose 7 percent instead, even though current polls put NasDem at only 4 percent. Non-parliamentary parties rejected the 5 percent figure entirely, with Labor Party president Said Iqbal calling it political colonialism and arguing it violated Constitutional Court guidelines. Experts warned that 17 million votes were wasted in 2024 under the 4 percent threshold, and raising it would disenfranchise even more voters.

Indonesia's first aircraft carrier, the former Italian vessel Giuseppe Garibaldi, arrived in Jakarta on September 18 to be commissioned as KRI Sriwijaya.

The carrier entered Indonesian waters on September 17 and was escorted by two Indonesian frigates before docking at Tanjung Priok Port at 7:58 a.m. on September 18. Italy transferred the 41-year-old, 180-meter vessel free of charge, though Indonesia faces between €350 million and €818 million in reactivation and modernization costs. The Indonesian Navy renamed it KRI Sriwijaya after the historic Sumatran maritime empire, reversing an earlier plan to call it KRI Gajah Mada. A 400-member crew has been prepared, including 100 sailors who sailed the vessel from Italy alongside Italian personnel. Navy Chief Muhammad Ali said the carrier, which can accommodate 18 helicopters and 830 personnel, will focus on disaster relief operations and is expected to be featured at the October 5 Armed Forces Day celebrations.

President Prabowo conducted his sixth cabinet reshuffle in two years, replacing Finance Minister Purbaya after internal tensions over personnel rotations.

Prabowo removed Purbaya Yudhi Sadewa on September 14 after just one year in office, appointing Deputy Finance Minister Suahasil Nazara as his replacement. Purbaya acknowledged on September 15 that his decision to reshuffle approximately 400 ministry officials on September 10 was partly responsible for his dismissal. The rotations, which affected senior tax officials and other key positions, reportedly triggered prolonged internal tensions within the Finance Ministry. The Finance Ministry announced it would review the personnel rotations after Nazara consulted with President Prabowo. The dismissal marked the sixth reshuffle since Prabowo took office in October 2024, continuing a pattern that has seen him replace ministers in finance, higher education, political affairs, and youth sectors.

Writing in The Diplomat, Sebastian Strangio reports that President Prabowo Subianto has replaced Finance Minister Purbaya Yudhi Sadewa with longtime deputy Suahasil Nazara amid growing investor skepticism about Indonesia's fiscal trajectory. The removal follows a turbulent year marked by rupiah depreciation, credit rating downgrades from Moody's and Fitch citing policy uncertainty, and the threatened MSCI downgrade to frontier market status. Nazara, who has served as deputy since 2019, was instructed to safeguard the health and credibility of the state budget, signaling an attempt to reassure markets concerned about Prabowo's expansive spending agenda including his multibillion-dollar free meal program.

Writing in The Diplomat, Sebastian Strangio reports that Indonesian lawmakers are debating potential changes to the country's fiscal deficit ceiling, with coalition members arguing the 3 percent GDP limit should not be treated as rigid. Commission XI chairman Mukhamad Misbakhun proposed establishing conditions under which larger deficits could be permitted, such as weak tax revenue or rising energy subsidies, to support Prabowo's ambitious welfare policies and 8 percent growth target. The debate matters because the 2003 State Finance Law was designed to reassure foreign investors after the Asian financial crisis, and any weakening could further alarm institutional investors already concerned about Indonesia's fiscal discipline.

Writing in Fulcrum, Burhanuddin Muhtadi demonstrates through statistical analysis that Nahdlatul Ulama is not a monolithic voting bloc, with its numerical strength never guaranteeing consolidated electoral support. The 2024 election exemplified this fragmentation, as Nahdliyin support split dramatically between candidates, with Prabowo-Gibran winning broad backing despite lacking ideological connection to NU, largely through Jokowi's mobilizing power. Muhtadi argues that understanding NU's electoral influence requires distinguishing between cultural proximity and active organizational membership, with the latter proving more predictive of voting behavior than simple identification with NU traditions.

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Fia Hamid-Walker argues that Presidential Instruction 11 of 2026 on forest fires does more than strengthen enforcement—it reorganises who has authority over fire governance in Indonesia. The regulation redistributes power by giving the central government stronger standard-setting and supervisory roles, requiring regional governments to operate within tighter national constraints, and mandating that communities practicing customary slash-and-burn make those practices legible through verification and registration. This reconfiguration of authority, including requiring the Coordinating Minister to report on regional regulations within 30 days, may prove as consequential as the instruction's tougher prevention measures.
