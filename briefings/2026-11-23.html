What Happened Last Week

President Prabowo Subianto is expected to submit two vice presidential candidates to parliament on November 25 to replace Gibran Rakabuming Raka, who resigned earlier this month.

Vice President Gibran Rakabuming Raka resigned on November 12 following a constitutional court ruling that confirmed he lacked proof of high school graduation, leaving the vice presidential position vacant. The MPR formally requested two names from Prabowo on November 13, giving him 14 days to propose candidates. By Friday, PAN chairman Zulkifli Hasan said nearly all coalition party leaders had agreed not to nominate their own cadres, instead supporting a candidate from Prabowo's Gerindra Party. Palace officials confirmed Prabowo will present two names on Wednesday, November 25, though former minister Mahfud MD warned that the MPR cannot validly hold an inauguration session on December 9 unless the constitutional 14-day notice period is met. The MPR has established three working groups to verify candidates and prepare for the selection process.

Former police chief Listyo Sigit Prabowo remained in detention this week following his November 3 arrest for allegedly wiretapping President Prabowo and dozens of other officials.

Listyo Sigit Prabowo, who served as national police chief for five years before becoming presidential chief of staff in October, was arrested on November 3 alongside two other senior officers for allegedly illegally wiretapping the president's phone between 2025 and 2026. President Prabowo dismissed him as chief of staff the following day, with senior minister Yusril Ihza Mahendra suggesting the case could amount to treason. Police chief Suyudi Ario Seto revealed to parliament that Listyo and his associates also intercepted communications of KPK officials, governors, and activists, with Listyo detained until November 23. The scandal accelerated Prabowo's efforts to dismantle his political accommodation with predecessor Joko Widodo, removing Widodo-aligned ministers in an October cabinet reshuffle. By Friday, the presidential communications office reported that public satisfaction with Prabowo had surged from 43 percent to 62-63 percent following the arrests.

Indonesia and the European Union signed the IEU-CEPA trade agreement in Brussels on November 21, eliminating tariffs on over 90 percent of bilateral trade.

Trade Minister Budi Santoso and European Commissioner Maroš Šefčovič signed the IEU-CEPA in Brussels on Saturday, concluding a decade of negotiations that survived disputes over palm oil and nickel exports. Coordinating Minister for Economic Affairs Airlangga Hartarto simultaneously signed an Investment Protection Agreement covering legal protections for investors between the two partners. The agreement, described by Santoso as Indonesia's most modern and comprehensive trade agreement, will eliminate Indonesia's 50 percent car import duty within five years while opening EU markets to Indonesian textiles, footwear, and agricultural products. Tariff eliminations take effect January 1, 2027, with Hartarto calling the pact a "game changer" covering market liberalization, the digital economy, and investment in electric vehicles and renewables. The signing came as President Prabowo attended the APEC summit in Shenzhen, where he held bilateral meetings with Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin to discuss economic partnerships and regional cooperation.

Expert Commentary This Week

Writing in Fulcrum, Yanuar Nugroho argues that Gibran Rakabuming Raka's resignation as vice-president represents consolidation that leaves fewer institutions able to balance presidential power, completing a pattern alongside Prabowo's cabinet reshuffles. The changes signal that the logic of presidential prerogative has now reached high offices meant to stand apart from the presidency, including the national police and the vice presidency itself. What matters is not how much more power Prabowo holds, but that institutional capacity to scrutinize and restrain the president has been systematically narrowed, deepening what scholars call party cartelization as coalition partners trade opposition for state resource access.

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Ahmad Rizky M Umar argues that Prabowo's presidentialisation of foreign policy means domestic crises now directly affect Indonesia's international relations, with the president notably absent from recent diplomatic engagements following the wiretapping scandal and Gibran's resignation. Unlike previous administrations where foreign ministers led diplomacy independently, Prabowo appointed his protégé as the first non-career diplomat foreign minister in 25 years and personally controlled diplomatic initiatives. The analysis suggests this personalization has created exhaustion in Indonesia's foreign policy, evidenced by Prabowo's withdrawal from the UN General Assembly and limited engagement at the BRICS summit despite his earlier enthusiasm for membership.

Writing in East Asia Forum, Vedi Hadiz argues that the 2026 conflict between Indonesia's police and Attorney General's Office over investigating former deputy attorney general Febrie Adriansyah exposes the limits of institutional tinkering in contexts where corruption control has become an instrument of accumulation and coalition-building. The case reveals how Reformasi-era institutional autonomy constrains presidential efforts to recentralize power, with competing law enforcement agencies representing rival oligarchic networks within Prabowo's coalition. The confrontation reflects deeper material tensions that cannot be resolved administratively because discretionary powers from anti-corruption investigations themselves provide opportunities for predatory practices among powerful political networks.

Writing in The Diplomat, James Guild argues there is a disconnect between the vibes and the data in Indonesia's economy, where GDP growth remains steady at 5 percent and major banks generate billions in profits while the stock market crashes and public anxiety about costs rises. The largest listed companies like Bank Mandiri and Bank Rakyat Indonesia recorded huge profits and paid over 5 billion dollars in dividends, yet their shares trade at very low valuations amid sustained market sell-offs. Guild attributes this paradox to credibility and governance concerns, as government policy uncertainty and questions about equitable resource distribution undermine investor confidence despite solid economic fundamentals.
