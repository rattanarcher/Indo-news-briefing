What Happened Last Week

Indonesia's Constitutional Court ruled that the flagship free nutritious meals program must be separated from the education budget by 2028.

The court handed down the decision on Thursday, declaring that free meals are not a primary component of education and ordering the government to create a separate budget line no later than the 2028 fiscal year. The ruling partially granted petitions from students and educators who challenged the 2026 budget law, which had allocated 223 trillion rupiah of the 769 trillion rupiah education budget to the free meals initiative. The National Nutrition Agency pledged compliance on Friday, while presidential spokesperson Prasetyo Hadi confirmed the government would respect the verdict and deliberate with lawmakers on implementation. The decision struck at the heart of President Prabowo Subianto's main campaign promise, though the court allowed the current 2026 budget allocation to remain valid.

President Prabowo's electability plunged from 34.9 percent to 14.5 percent over nine months, according to a survey released Thursday.

Saiful Mujani Research and Consulting showed West Java Governor Dedi Mulyadi overtaking the incumbent with 35 percent support in a semi-open simulation conducted July 5-19. In a head-to-head matchup, Dedi led Prabowo 59 percent to 28.3 percent. SMRC attributed the decline to deteriorating public evaluations of government performance on the economy, law enforcement, and programs including the free meals initiative. Gerindra chairman Dasco Ahmad responded calmly on Friday, calling fluctuations normal, while the palace on Saturday maintained there were no cabinet reshuffle plans. The pattern contrasted sharply with previous presidents Jokowi and SBY, whose electability remained stable or increased during comparable early periods in office.

Kaesang Pangarep declared he would run for parliament from Central Java's fifth district in the 2029 elections.

The PSI chairman announced his candidacy during a party coordination meeting in Solo on Saturday, July 25, targeting a constituency covering Solo, Boyolali, Sukoharjo, and Klaten—the same territory held by DPR Speaker Puan Maharani. Analysts identified three factors supporting his bid: the influence of his father, former President Jokowi, whose political base is centered in Solo; appeal to younger Gen Z voters; and his majority ownership of Persis Solo football club. PDI-P secretary general Hasto Kristiyanto responded coolly on Tuesday, calling it a normal political process, though the matchup sets up a direct contest between the Jokowi and Megawati political families in what has long been considered PDI-P's stronghold.

Expert Commentary This Week

Writing in The Diplomat, Sebastian Strangio reports that Bank Indonesia Governor Perry Warjiyo's unexpected resignation has intensified concerns about political interference in economic policymaking under President Prabowo Subianto. The resignation comes as the central bank faces pressure to support Prabowo's ambitious 8 percent annual growth target and high-spending populist policies that have widened the fiscal deficit and unsettled investors already worried about the administration's economic stewardship. The departure follows the earlier firing of Finance Minister Sri Mulyani Indrawati and recent credit rating downgrades, compounding doubts about Indonesia's commitment to fiscal conservatism at a time when the rupiah has fallen to its lowest level since the 1997-1998 Asian Financial Crisis.

Writing in The Diplomat, Muhammad Rauhan Rasheed and Sarmad Naeem analyze Indonesia's plans to acquire Chinese-made J-10C fighters and potentially JF-17s from Pakistan, representing an unusually active form of hedging that could fragment its air force and create regional uncertainty. The authors note that while Indonesia's strategy of diversifying military suppliers across China, France, Turkey and others makes political sense for preserving diplomatic flexibility, it creates practical challenges for building a coherent air force. This military hedging mirrors Indonesia's broader approach of simultaneously joining BRICS, deepening ties with China on economic and security fronts, while also participating in Western-led exercises and engaging in maritime rivalry with Beijing over the Natuna Islands.

Writing in The Diplomat, James Guild examines Indonesia's Whoosh high-speed rail, which continues operating at losses approaching under $300 million a year absorbed by state-owned companies in the joint venture. Guild argues that while Indonesia's government can clearly afford this amount and the losses do not pose systemic fiscal risk, the real question is where those losses should be allocated rather than continuing to burden companies like state railway operator KAI. The project, delivered late and over budget at over $7 billion by Chinese firms and financed through China Development Bank, raises questions about proper use of public funds amid mounting fiscal pressures, though Guild notes other transport modes are already heavily subsidized.
