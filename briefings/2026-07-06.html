What Happened Last Week

Five trainees died during military drills for village cooperative managers, prompting Indonesia to scale back the program amid criticism it militarized civilian roles.

Five prospective managers of the Red and White Cooperatives program died between June 17 and June 26 during a 45-day training course that required 35,476 participants to complete 30 days of basic military training before managing President Prabowo Subianto's flagship village cooperatives. The Defense Ministry linked the deaths to cardiac arrest, heat stroke, tuberculosis and pneumonia, prompting lawmakers and activists to demand a transparent investigation. Human Rights Minister Natalius Pigai said character-building "does not have to be done through a military system," while Indonesia's National Commission on Human Rights urged the government to end basic military training for cooperative managers altogether. By the end of the week, Indonesia had scaled back its basic military training for prospective managers, with the Defense Ministry changing the program to state-defense and managerial preparation starting in July, reducing physical intensity for the civilian trainees.

Former President Jokowi launched a political tour with PSI across Lampung and announced plans for Central Java, fueling speculation about the 2029 elections.

Jokowi arrived in Lampung on June 26 and spent three days visiting transmigrant enclaves, wearing PSI-logoed shirts and hats while attending party coordination meetings and meeting thousands of PSI cadres in districts including Mesuji and Tulang Bawang. At one event, Jokowi told supporters that PSI's organizational structure in Lampung had reached more than 90 percent at the branch level, calling it "a foundation to make PSI a major party" if members worked hard. Following Lampung, PSI Jawa Tengah scheduled a safari covering all 35 districts and cities in Central Java for mid-July 2026, with party leaders pledging to prove that Central Java is truly the "elephant's cage"—a reference to PSI's new elephant logo. Political analyst Adi Prayitno said the safari aims to get PSI into parliament, while PDI-P's Puan Maharani reminded parties to keep the political situation stable amid the competition.

Papuan separatists shot and killed American pilot Nicholas Goselin and burned his aircraft on July 2, escalating conflict in Indonesia's restive highlands.

The West Papua Liberation Army shot Nicholas F. Goselin and set fire to a PT AMA aircraft in Balinggama village in Yahukimo regency shortly after he landed. The separatist group's spokesman alleged the aircraft had been "frequently dropping Indonesian military personnel and violating the TPNPB's ultimatum," though the Indonesian military denied the claim and said seven Indigenous Papuan passengers were unharmed. The group said "the shooting of the American pilot is the result of the failure of the Indonesian, U.S. and Dutch governments, as well as the United Nations, to address the root causes of the conflict in Papua" and called for international negotiations. The Indonesian military evacuated Goselin's body on July 3 using a 10-person unit supported by two helicopters, and security forces said personnel were pursuing those responsible. Goselin had worked for Grant Aviation in Alaska for more than five years before joining the mission aviation company in Papua.

Expert Commentary This Week

Writing in The Diplomat, James Guild argues that Indonesia's current market turmoil reflects a fundamental tension between state and market that has existed for decades, now taking on new dimensions under Prabowo. While Jokowi also pursued interventionist policies that rattled investors, his competent technocrats and focus on infrastructure helped markets accept his approach, whereas Prabowo's policies face greater skepticism amid concerns about investment climate uncertainty and unsustainable spending. Guild suggests the key issue is not whether the state intervenes, but rather the degree, purpose, and market response to that intervention.

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Muhammad Maulidan argues that Prabowo's single-door export policy for palm oil, coal, and ferroalloys represents a critical shift where the Indonesian state has shifted its primary role from regulator to a single gateway controlling all export flows through state-owned PT Danantara. Drawing parallels to Suharto's failed clove monopoly under the BPPC in the 1990s, which enriched elites while harming farmers and ultimately collapsed under its own inefficiency, Maulidan warns that such buffer agencies have troubling precedents. Markets responded negatively to the announcement, with the stock index plunging over 2 percent as investors sensed the return of New Order-style monopolies.

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Ryan Eka Permana Sakti argues that the ten-year prison sentence for former education minister and Gojek co-founder Nadiem Makarim demonstrates how Indonesia's broadly-worded Anti-Corruption Law means no honest government official can feel safe from prosecution. The conviction, based on state loss calculations from auditor assumptions rather than market surveys, exemplifies how routine administrative decisions during pandemic-era emergencies can be retrospectively criminalized without proof of corrupt intent. This unpredictability, extending beyond just one case to a pattern of politically charged prosecutions, threatens Indonesia's judicial credibility and regulatory trust in ways that presidential pardons cannot resolve.
