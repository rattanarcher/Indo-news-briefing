What Happened Last Week

President Prabowo dismissed his chief of staff Listyo Sigit Prabowo after police named him a suspect in allegedly wiretapping the president and other senior officials.

On November 3, police announced Listyo Sigit and two other senior officers as suspects in an alleged illegal wiretapping operation targeting President Prabowo Subianto and other prominent figures. The unauthorized surveillance reportedly took place from 2025 through 2026 while Listyo served as national police chief, a position he held for five years before becoming presidential chief of staff in October. Prabowo fired Listyo on November 4, with government officials suggesting the wiretapping could amount to treason. Police named a fourth suspect, Inspector General Slamet Uliandi, on November 5, and by week's end investigators were probing who ordered the surveillance operation. The scandal exposed deepening cracks between Prabowo and his predecessor Joko Widodo, with Listyo widely known as a Jokowi loyalist, though Jokowi himself declined to comment beyond saying he would respect the legal process.

Indonesia's military deployed more than 20,000 troops to Jakarta in a four-phase operation as the wiretapping scandal unfolded.

A widely circulated TNI document detailed plans to mobilize nearly 21,000 Navy personnel to reinforce security in the capital under Jakarta Military Command control. The deployment began November 2 with 2,100 personnel from Jakarta-based units, followed by additional phases bringing in troops from regional commands across Java and Lampung using land routes and Navy vessels. TNI officials characterized the deployment as routine preparedness, with Head of TNI Information Center Maj. Gen. Muhammad Nas stating the military "must always be prepared to protect the entire nation." Authorities did not officially confirm a link to the wiretapping investigation, though military analyst Aris Santoso suggested the Marine Corps deployment was likely intended to prepare for possible political tensions following Listyo's arrest. Jakarta Governor Pramono Anung said strengthening security in the capital is the central government's responsibility and the provincial government would provide support.

New national police chief Suyudi Ario Seto conducted five major personnel mutations within his first month, reassigning hundreds of officers across all levels.

Kapolri Suyudi executed five waves of mutations since his October 1 inauguration, with the fifth round on November 2 reshuffling 264 senior and mid-level officers including six regional deputy police chiefs. Police spokesman Irjen Pol Johnny Eddizon Isir said the mutations were part of organizational dynamics and career development to ensure leadership continuity amid increasingly complex policing challenges. Observers characterized the rapid-fire personnel changes as a sharp break from the previous leadership, with security analyst Bambang Rukminto calling Suyudi's approach a "radical revolution" following five years under Listyo Sigit's command. The sweeping restructuring touched positions from headquarters to regional commands, with 224 of the 264 officers receiving promotions or equivalent-level assignments.

The authors explain that while Indonesia's proportional representation system necessitates broad coalitions for presidential viability, Prabowo's big-tent approach has blurred lines of authority and fostered friction between coalition members. They warn that the 2029 general election and ongoing discussions on electoral law revisions could test coalition cohesion and internal party dynamics.

Writing in East Asia Forum, Hening Wikan argues that the rolling blackouts across Java in June 2026 exposed critical vulnerabilities in Indonesia's electricity system stemming from heavy reliance on coal, particularly when rising global energy prices divert domestic supply. The analysis highlights that a significant gap between regulated domestic coal prices capped at $70 per tonne and international prices reaching over $121 per tonne reduces commercial incentives for producers to prioritize domestic energy supply despite minimum allocation requirements. Wikan emphasizes that strengthening energy security will require diversifying the electricity mix to reduce dependence on a single source and improve system resilience.

Writing in The Diplomat, Bianca Febriola argues that while Indonesia's navy has reached a milestone by commissioning its first aircraft carrier and expanding its fleet with new surface combatants and submarines, true naval strength depends on how effectively platforms are equipped, operated, and integrated rather than fleet size alone. Febriola notes that Indonesia's geographic position as an archipelagic state with 6.4 million square kilometers of maritime territory and proximity to strategic shipping routes like the Strait of Malacca requires sustained operational reach and readiness across multiple areas. She emphasizes that evolving maritime security challenges, including unmanned systems, long-range missiles, and cyber capabilities, demand increasingly integrated detection, protection, and response capabilities.

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Vindi Kurniawan argues that Indonesian citizens' positive reception of a Malaysian cartoon's haze-awareness message represents a new expression of patriotism through demanding government accountability rather than defending the government's reputation during cross-border crises. Kurniawan highlights that this response stands in stark contrast to the two nations' history of rivalry, with Indonesians supporting planned Malaysian protests and welcoming what they perceive as criticism of Jakarta's handling of forest fires in Sumatra and Kalimantan. The analysis shows that foreign criticism can give domestic grievances another hearing, particularly amid confusion over official fire data and declining satisfaction with President Prabowo's performance.
