What Happened Last Week

Nanik Deyang resigned as head of Indonesia's National Nutrition Agency after 44 days, citing heart treatment, and was replaced by former deputy agriculture minister Sudaryono.

Nanik announced her resignation on Wednesday morning, July 22, saying she would seek medical treatment abroad for a heart condition. President Prabowo Subianto swore in Sudaryono as the new BGN chief that same afternoon, selecting him because he had been involved in designing the Free Nutritious Meals (MBG) program from the outset. The new chief denied owning any program kitchens and pledged zero tolerance for corruption in the high-budget initiative. State Secretary Prasetyo Hadi said Prabowo asked Nanik to continue assisting with BGN oversight, though the agency's regulations only provide for an advisory council, not the supervisory board role Nanik had mentioned. The shuffle drew scrutiny from opposition lawmakers, with DPR Speaker Puan Maharani urging performance improvements and stronger accountability in the free meals program.

Armed separatists in Papua's Yahukimo district killed three indigenous civilians on July 20, accusing them of intelligence work, and security forces evacuated two bodies by week's end.

The West Papua National Liberation Army claimed responsibility for the shootings near the Trans Papua Highway in Seradala subdistrict, with victims identified as a married couple and another woman from the Unaukam tribe. TPNPB spokesman Sebby Sambom said the group executed the civilians because they were accused of being intelligence agents for security forces, though Indonesia's military information chief strongly denied the allegation. A joint military-police team evacuated two bodies to Dekai hospital by Thursday, with the operation hampered by strong currents in the Brazza River. Yahukimo police chief Zet Saalino said the team would resume searching Friday to evacuate the third victim, a woman, while the military said overall security in Papua Highlands remained stable despite the violence. The attack, led by commander Kopitua Heluka, underscored continuing separatist activity in a region that has seen previous massacres of gold miners in the same district.

Foreign Minister Sugiono pressed Japan and ASEAN partners to strengthen support for Palestine during a week of meetings in Manila tied to the 59th ASEAN ministerial summit.

At the ASEAN-Japan foreign ministers meeting on July 22, Sugiono called for optimizing the CEAPAD forum to provide concrete support for Palestine's recovery and development, describing Palestine as a matter of justice and dignity. Indonesia pledged $2 million in humanitarian aid through the ICRC for Gaza during the fifth CEAPAD ministerial meeting on July 23, while emphasizing the need for sustainable international support to build an independent Palestinian state. Sugiono also welcomed the UK's recognition of Palestine during the ASEAN-UK meeting, urging the permanent Security Council member to match recognition with protection of civilians and humanitarian access. On the summit's sidelines, he held a bilateral meeting with Palestinian Social Development Minister Samah Hamad, reiterating Indonesia's commitment through both diplomatic backing and capacity-building for Palestinian institutions. The meetings underscored Indonesia's sustained advocacy for Palestine as ASEAN navigated broader regional challenges including maritime security and economic cooperation.

Expert Commentary This Week

Writing in Fulcrum, Chatib Basri and co-authors argue that empirical evidence demonstrates a shrinking middle class in Indonesia has led to growing numbers of protests, with demonstrations at the district level increasing sharply since 2018. The authors' analysis shows that between 2018 and 2025, fewer workers earned middle-class livelihoods while more educated workers moved into informal employment, a pattern coinciding with rising street demonstrations. This nexus matters because the middle class, characterized as professional complainers who are more proactive in voicing aspirations and less fearful of criticizing government, appear to be driving increased social unrest as their economic position deteriorates.

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Muhammad Zulfikar Rakhmat argues that President Prabowo's reported practice of personally covering costs when official foreign trips exceed state budgets represents a dangerous personalisation of the state rather than admirable generosity. Rakhmat contends that foreign policy is a fundamental sovereign responsibility that belongs to 280 million Indonesians, not a private hobby to be subsidized by individual wealth, and that such arrangements blur the institutional line between public office and private fortune. He suggests that if diplomatic trips are truly in the national interest they deserve transparent public funding through constitutional processes, while questioning whether the sheer frequency of Prabowo's travel actually constitutes effective diplomacy.

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Rafiqa Qurrata Ayun argues that the Indonesian Ulama Council's draft bill to criminalize LGBTQ activities gains dangerous legitimacy from President Prabowo's Presidential Regulation classifying LGBTQ people as a non-military threat to national defense. This securitization framework, absent from predecessor regulations under Jokowi, creates state apparatus for constructing LGBTQ individuals as threats to sovereignty and territorial integrity, risking strengthened anti-LGBTQ sentiments and supporting criminalization efforts. Ayun warns that the regulation's ambiguity and involvement of quasi-state institutions like MUI in combating vaguely defined threats historically used to persecute critics offers little comfort despite official assurances against persecution.
