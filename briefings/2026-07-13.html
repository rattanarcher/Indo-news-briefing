What Happened Last Week

Indonesia sent Foreign Minister Sugiono to Iran's holy city of Mashhad to attend the burial of Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei amid escalating U.S.-Iran military strikes.

The funeral became a test of Indonesia's foreign policy after critics charged that sending only the ambassador reflected Jakarta tilting toward Washington. Sugiono and MPR Speaker Ahmad Muzani flew to Mashhad on July 9 and paid respects at the burial site on Friday, July 10, where Khamenei was interred beside Imam Reza. The visit occurred as Indonesia urged both sides to de-escalate, with Foreign Ministry spokesperson Yvonne Mewengkang calling on July 9 for maximum restraint after the U.S. struck over 80 Iranian targets and Iran launched retaliatory missile attacks on American bases in Kuwait and Bahrain. President Trump declared at the NATO summit in Ankara that the memorandum of understanding with Iran was "over", though reports later surfaced that Washington had paused strikes to allow diplomacy to work.

Indonesia and India signed a defense deal for BrahMos supersonic cruise missiles and Astra air-to-air missiles during Modi's state visit to Jakarta.

President Prabowo and Prime Minister Modi announced the agreement at Merdeka Palace on Tuesday, July 7, with sources putting the package near $630 million. The BrahMos procurement, a joint Indo-Russian product, makes Indonesia the third foreign buyer after the Philippines and Vietnam. A separate cooperation pact between Bharat Dynamics and Indonesia's Republikorp covers air-to-air missile systems. India's government said the deals aim to boost Indonesia's self-reliance in defense manufacturing. Modi was greeted at Halim Perdanakusuma Air Force Base by Prabowo, underscoring the importance of the visit, and addressed Parliament before traveling to Yogyakarta on Wednesday.

Prabowo inaugurated five dams across four provinces on Friday, projects the government expects to add one million tons of rice production annually.

Speaking at the main ceremony at Meninting Dam in West Lombok, the president opened the Meninting, Rukoh, Keureuto, Jlantah, and Sidan dams in West Nusa Tenggara, Aceh, Central Java, and Bali. Coordinating Minister AHY revealed the projects represent a combined state investment of Rp9.7 trillion (US$536.62 million). Prabowo said the agriculture minister reported that with technology and superior seed varieties, the five dams could yield an additional one million tons of rice. The president acknowledged that construction began between 2015 and 2025 under previous administrations, and urged authorities to ensure farmers receive the water they need. He linked the infrastructure push to the B50 biofuel launch the previous day, framing both as steps toward national self-sufficiency.

Expert Commentary This Week

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Gregory Sulaeman argues that managed coercion is reshaping Indonesia's political-economic order as President Prabowo Subianto transitions from Jokowi's coordinated developmentalism to a more directive system of state-led governance. Through the creation of Danantara Sumberdaya Indonesia, a new state-owned enterprise with monopoly control over strategic commodity exports like coal and palm oil, Prabowo is extracting compliance from capital by leveraging their structural dependence on the executive. The evaluation phase for existing contracts functions as a powerful oversight tool, dismantling commercial confidentiality and forcing conglomerates into complete corporate transparency before the state, fundamentally altering the balance of power between state and capital in Indonesia's political economy.

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Norman Joshua contends that Prabowo is advancing his agenda of centralisation by extending state control over the movement of goods and capital through the creation of PT Danantara Sumberdaya Indonesia as a single export channel for key commodities. While Jokowi's hilirisasi agenda sought to capture value by dictating where commodities are processed, Prabowo seeks to control the flow itself by positioning the state as sole intermediary for resource sales. This centralising logic is evident across sectors, from forcing private fuel retailers to buy exclusively from Pertamina to granting DSI sweeping powers to set export prices and profit margins, marking a fundamental shift in Indonesia's economic statecraft that favours state control over market mechanisms.

Writing in Fulcrum, Made Supriatma argues that President Prabowo's replacement of the National Nutrition Agency head does little to address the deeper structural problems plaguing the Free Nutritious Meals programme, which consumes nearly seven percent of Indonesia's state budget. The corruption charges against Dadan Hindayana and his deputies for contract rigging and procurement markups, combined with the appointment of an inexperienced journalist to lead the agency, highlight fundamental design and implementation flaws. Despite budget cuts and programmatic refocusing on frontier regions and vulnerable populations, the leadership changes and target shifts fail to resolve the institutional weaknesses that enabled such widespread mismanagement.
