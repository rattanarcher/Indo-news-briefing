What Happened Last Week

Seventy-nine local governments face payroll shortfalls requiring Rp 3.7 trillion, prompting the central government to offer a three-step solution.

Home Affairs Minister Tito Karnavian revealed on Thursday that 79 regional governments need between Rp 3.5 and Rp 3.7 trillion to cover employee payroll costs, with total budget shortfalls reaching up to Rp 14 trillion when other expenses are included. The fiscal strain emerged following the recent hiring of contract government workers under the PPPK scheme. Tito outlined three steps to address the crisis: requiring regions to pursue budget efficiency first, accelerating revenue-sharing transfers for resource-rich areas, and providing top-up funding from the Finance Ministry only as a last resort for regions that have exhausted other options. By week's end, Finance Minister Purbaya Yudhi Sadewa announced the government would disburse Rp 20.5 trillion in aid to 490 local governments to maintain regional financial stability and ensure timely salary payments.

The Constitutional Court ruled that the Free Nutritious Meals program budget must be separated from the education allocation by 2028.

In a decision read on July 30, the court granted part of a petition challenging the inclusion of the flagship MBG program in the mandatory 20-percent education budget, finding that it created uncertainty about actual education spending. The ruling, issued as Decision Number 40/PUU-XXIV/2026, allows MBG to continue using education funds in 2026 but requires full separation no later than the 2028 state budget, though the court encouraged earlier implementation in 2027. Early in the week, DPR Commission X chair Hetifah Sjaifudian called on the government to identify alternative funding sources such as new budget allocations or reallocation from non-education sectors. Finance Minister Purbaya pledged compliance with the decision, while the National Nutrition Agency confirmed it would follow official directives to implement the program within regulatory constraints.

Indonesia cut first steel for its domestically-built Scorpene submarine at PT PAL's Surabaya shipyard on August 7.

The steel-cutting ceremony formally opened physical construction of the first of two submarines ordered under the Scorpène Republik Indonesia program, with delivery targeted for 2032. PT PAL will handle hull construction, equipment integration, testing, and final assembly, while France's Naval Group provides design and technical support under a comprehensive technology transfer arrangement. The milestone followed successful training sessions for Indonesian specialists in Cherbourg, France, and certification phases validating the shipyard's workforce and facilities. PT PAL CEO Kaharuddin Djenod emphasized the program aims not merely to build two submarines but to master submarine technology that will enable Indonesia to design and build future vessels independently, with construction of the second boat expected to begin in 2027.

Expert Commentary This Week

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Elita Pertiwi examines why university graduates are increasingly competing for janitorial positions in Jakarta's Public Facility Management Agency, with over 7,000 applicants for 1,023 positions requiring only primary-school certificates. She reports that overeducation has become the norm in urban Indonesia, with six in ten urban Gen Z wage earners holding bachelor qualifications employed in jobs below their education level. The phenomenon mirrors the trend of graduates working as online motorcycle-taxi drivers, both reflecting a labour market where accessing formal employment with social protection has become fiercely competitive while educational qualifications no longer guarantee appropriate work.

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Annisa Dina Amalia analyzes Indonesia's proposed limited dual citizenship scheme restricted to researchers, scientists, specialists, and athletes nominated by state agencies, arguing it reveals selective prioritization of whose multiple belonging is deemed valuable enough to formalize. She contrasts the state's enthusiasm for naturalizing foreign footballers with thin genealogical ties against its reluctance to extend comparable flexibility to millions of overseas Indonesians with lifelong attachments, noting the proposal excludes migrant workers whose remittances have long sustained the economy. The policy, emerging amid concerns about brain drain and a fivefold increase in citizenship renunciation fees, demonstrates that Indonesia's discomfort is not with multiple nationality itself but with which diaspora segments warrant official recognition.
