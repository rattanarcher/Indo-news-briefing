What Happened Last Week

Indonesia pledged to restructure its flagship Free Nutritious Meals program within one month amid mounting corruption allegations and budget inefficiencies.

The government's signature MBG program came under intense scrutiny last week after the number of meal kitchens ballooned to 27,877, nearly 7,000 more than the planned 21,000 sites. Coordinating Minister for Food Affairs Zulkifli Hasan committed to overhauling the program within one month on Thursday, while reports emerged of budget waste reaching up to 1 trillion rupiah per month. State Secretary Minister Prasetyo Hadi said Friday that the government expects lower budget requirements following governance improvements, with the restructuring targeted for completion within a month. The 2026 MBG budget had already been cut from Rp335 trillion to Rp268 trillion in May following President Prabowo's directive to improve efficiency. By Sunday, officials announced plans to reassess recipients, including removing students at elite schools and redirecting assistance to remote regions.

Iran's closure of the Strait of Hormuz stranded two Indonesian Pertamina tankers as Jakarta offered to mediate between Washington and Tehran.

Iran announced on Friday it had closed the Strait of Hormuz indefinitely following renewed military clashes with the United States in the Persian Gulf. Deputy Foreign Minister Arif Havas Oegroseno said Thursday that Indonesia was maintaining communication with Iran to secure the release of two Pertamina oil tankers—the Pertamina Pride and the Gamsunoro—currently trapped in the strait. Deputy Foreign Minister Arrmanatha Nasir told reporters Friday that Indonesia's offer to mediate the conflict remains open, reflecting President Prabowo's commitment to helping end the tensions. Meanwhile, international reports indicated that US-Iran peace negotiations were advancing, with Pakistan's Prime Minister announcing Saturday that the two sides had reached a final agreed-upon text. President Trump stated Sunday that the deal would be signed imminently, though uncertainties remained over the stranded tankers' safe passage.

Parliament passed a controversial police law allowing active officers to hold civilian government posts and extending the retirement age.

The House of Representatives ratified the Police Bill on Tuesday, with lawmakers and the government agreeing that the retirement age for four-star officers would be set at 60 years, extendable by one year based on presidential decision. A key provision in Articles 28A and 29 allows active police members to occupy certain civil positions related to police functions without resigning from service. Critics including Amnesty International Indonesia warned the changes replicate what was done with the military law and represent "a sign of authoritarianism." The amendments also contradict a 2025 Constitutional Court ruling that required police officers to resign before taking positions outside the force, while the National Police Commission remains a consultative body without authority to punish errant officers.

Expert Commentary This Week

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Jefferson Ng argues that under Prabowo Subianto, the Indonesian military is becoming increasingly embedded in civilian policy implementation as an instrument of presidential rule. The administration plans to establish around 155 development battalions by 2026, with the long-term goal of placing at least one development battalion in every one of Indonesia's 500-plus regencies and cities, integrated into the territorial command structure inherited from the Soeharto era. These battalions, featuring specialized units for agriculture, healthcare, and construction, are driven by strategic defense concerns, crime reduction efforts, and state-led development objectives, effectively transforming the military into a vast instrument for centralized governance under direct presidential control.

Writing in Fulcrum, Siwage Dharma Negara and Leo Suryadinata argue that Indonesia's resource nationalism is testing its relationship with China as the interests of Chinese investors and the Prabowo administration become increasingly misaligned. Chinese firms, which have invested over 65 billion dollars in Indonesia's nickel industry, recently protested proposed royalty hikes, stricter foreign exchange rules, and reduced mining quotas that they say undermine investment certainty. The tensions reveal the limits of a partnership in which Indonesia seeks to extract greater returns from its mineral wealth while still depending heavily on Chinese capital to sustain its industrialization ambitions, particularly after the government established Danantara Sumberdaya Indonesia to oversee strategic natural resource exports including nickel products.

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Hellena Souisa argues that the documentary Pig Feast has become a test case for alternative media by filling the information drought on Papua that mainstream Indonesian media has created. The film, which garnered over 13 million YouTube views in 14 days, documents Indigenous resistance to a government food estate project that the filmmakers argue is actually a bioethanol venture causing massive deforestation and displacement. The documentary's widespread appeal reflects both public hunger for information about Papua and the failure of Indonesia's conglomerate-controlled mainstream media to cover marginalised communities and critical issues like environmental destruction and human rights abuses in the region.

Writing in The Diplomat, Alif Satria examines the future of the Indonesian military's involvement in counterterrorism following the release of a draft presidential regulation that drew strong criticism from civil society groups. The draft outlines three main functions for the TNI including prevention, enforcement, and recovery operations, but critics from a coalition of 21 organizations contend that the expansion of its role, combined with vague operational definitions, risks enabling overreach and creating unnecessary overlaps with existing counterterrorism units. Satria argues that future regulations should incorporate three key safeguards: limiting military involvement to high-threat environments, establishing clear coordination mechanisms, and ensuring strong oversight to address public fears about military overreach.
