What Happened Last Week

The Constitutional Court ruled it found no proof Vice President Gibran held a high school diploma but could not disqualify him from office.

Indonesia's Constitutional Court on Tuesday evening found no compelling evidence that Vice President Gibran Rakabuming Raka holds a high school diploma equivalent to support his 2024 candidacy, yet all nine justices dismissed the petition, saying his status as a candidate ended when he was inaugurated and the matter fell to other institutions. The court found no convincing evidence that documents submitted included a foreign diploma, only certificates of attendance and transcripts rather than proof of graduation. Golkar's Zulfikar Arse Sadikin said the ruling could not serve as the sole basis for impeachment, while calls for revising educational requirements for candidacy emerged across political circles. By week's end, Golkar announced support for Prabowo as its 2029 presidential candidate but left the vice-presidential slot open, signaling uncertainty about Gibran's political future. Former President Jokowi, once a vocal advocate for a "Prabowo-Gibran two terms" platform, notably stopped championing that slogan after the court decision.

Parliament passed the Labor Protection Law on October 6, fulfilling a Constitutional Court deadline and introducing new rules on contracts, severance, and oversight.

DPR unanimously approved the Labor Protection Law in a plenary session led by Speaker Puan Maharani on Tuesday, with labor confederation representatives applauding from the balcony as the bill became law. The 313-article legislation followed up on a 2024 Constitutional Court ruling that struck down labor provisions in the omnibus Cipta Kerja law and gave lawmakers until October 31, 2026 to enact a standalone labor statute. Labor Minister Yassierli called it a new era for labor law in Indonesia, strengthening enforcement with administrative and criminal sanctions for violations of worker protections. The law introduces 13 new provisions covering fixed-term contracts (PKWT), severance pay for layoffs, oversight committees, and integrated employment data systems, though business groups remained cautious and some worker advocates warned certain clauses could still disadvantage labor. The law will take effect after a one-year transition period from the date of promulgation.

National Police Chief Suyudi Ario Seto launched sweeping personnel changes one day after his October 1 inauguration, rotating 88 officers including 19 regional police chiefs.

Kapolri Suyudi moved swiftly on October 2 to reshuffle the force through a telegram ordering the reassignment of 88 senior and mid-level officers, with all five regional police chiefs on the island of Java replaced alongside 14 other top national headquarters positions. The October 2 telegram placed 70 officers in promotions or new assignments and elevated 21 personnel in rank, reshaping commands from Metro Jaya to Maluku, Riau, and beyond. Police spokesman Johnny described the changes as part of organizational dynamics aimed at refreshing leadership and meeting evolving policing demands, while observers said the true test would be whether the reshuffles translated into improved service and public trust. Suyudi also replaced the force's tagline from "Presisi" to "Polisi Bersama Masyarakat" (Police Together with the Community), signaling a shift in approach under his command.

Expert Commentary This Week

Writing in The Diplomat, Radityo Dharmaputra argues that Indonesia's current consular approach is inadequate to address the growing problem of citizens being recruited into Russia's army through deceptive labor migration schemes. Despite President Prabowo's efforts to build closer ties with Moscow, Indonesian institutions have promoted Russia as a labor destination even as agents lure citizens with promises of civilian jobs only to force them into military contracts they cannot read. The cases reveal that recruitment follows the logic of labor migration before turning into military service, with social media agents targeting both Indonesians at home and students already in Russia, leaving Jakarta unable to leverage Prabowo's personal relationship with Putin to protect trapped citizens.

Writing in Fulcrum, Suryaputra Wijaksana and Maria Monica Wihardja argue that Bank Indonesia now operates under constrained central bank independence following the 2023 P2SK law and its 2026 amendment. While the new governor Destry Damayanti's appointment calmed markets, the legal changes have fundamentally transformed BI from having a single mandate of price stability to a dual mandate that includes supporting economic growth and employment. The authors contend that without proper institutional safeguards, this shift pulls the central bank toward fiscal financing and political oversight, authorizing primary-market bond purchases during crises and allowing parliament to dismiss board members based on performance evaluations.
