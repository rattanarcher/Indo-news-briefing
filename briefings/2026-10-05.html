What Happened Last Week

President Prabowo carried out his seventh cabinet reshuffle on October 1st, replacing National Police Chief Listyo Sigit with Suyudi Ario Seto and rotating 16 senior positions.

On the morning of October 1st, Prabowo sent a presidential letter dismissing Listyo Sigit as National Police Chief after nearly six years in office. Parliament's Commission III unanimously approved Suyudi Ario Seto, the former head of the National Narcotics Agency, to replace him. By midday, Prabowo inaugurated 18 officials at the State Palace, including Listyo Sigit moving to Presidential Chief of Staff, replacing Dudung Abdurachman, who became the new Coordinating Minister for Political and Security Affairs. Foreign Minister Sugiono shifted to Coordinating Minister for Human Development and Culture, while career diplomat Arrmanatha Nasir took over the Foreign Ministry. Golkar Party chairman Bahlil Lahadalia became Coordinating Minister for Downstreaming and Energy Transition while retaining his energy portfolio, and Golkar secretary-general Muhammad Sarmuji was appointed Industry Minister. Analysts described the reshuffle as an effort to reinforce ties with coalition parties ahead of the 2029 elections.

New National Police Chief Suyudi Ario Seto launched a sweeping personnel shake-up just one day after taking office, rotating 88 officers including 19 regional police chiefs.

On Friday, October 2nd, one day after his inauguration, Suyudi issued a telegram ordering the mutation of 88 senior and mid-level police officers, with 70 receiving promotions or new assignments. The reshuffle completely overhauled the leadership of all six police regions across Java, including Metro Jakarta, West Java, Central Java, East Java, Yogyakarta, and Banten, alongside 13 other regional commands. At National Police Headquarters, Inspector General Asep Safrudin became the new Criminal Investigation Agency chief, Commissioner General Asep Edi Suheri moved from Metro Jakarta police chief to Inspector General, and Inspector General Dadang Hartanto was appointed as the new Metro Jakarta police chief. Police spokesman Johnny Eddizon justified the mutations as necessary for organizational needs and career refreshment, though civil society groups questioned the speed of the changes and lack of public consultation.

Prabowo appointed 26 members to the Presidential Advisory Council on October 2nd, including former Constitutional Court chief Anwar Usman and academic Rocky Gerung.

A day after the cabinet reshuffle, Prabowo inaugurated the new Wantimpres with former Coordinating Minister Pratikno as chair and former Coordinating Minister Hatta Rajasa as deputy chair. Among the 26 appointees, Rocky Gerung arrived carrying a blue tie, telling reporters he had a "radical break" event before entering the palace. The lineup also included Anwar Usman, the former Constitutional Court chief removed in 2023 for ethics violations after ruling to allow his nephew Gibran to run for vice president despite age requirements. Other appointees included Islamic scholar Quraish Shihab, businessman Chairul Tanjung, and former intelligence chief Budi Gunawan, who had been dismissed as Coordinating Minister in September 2025. After the swearing-in, Rocky explained that Prabowo emphasized Wantimpres held an "ethical" rather than technical position to provide foresight on policy consequences.

Expert Commentary This Week

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Abdil Mughis Mudhoffir argues that the rise and rise of the preman state explains the growing influence of violent groups like GRIB Jaya under President Prabowo Subianto. Led by former gangster Hercules Rosario, who has maintained ties with Prabowo since 1980s military operations in East Timor, GRIB Jaya was allegedly involved in deadly riots during August 2026 protests over the Asset Forfeiture Bill. Mudhoffir contends that such groups' impunity reflects Indonesia's structural character as a system where non-state violent actors are integral to political-economic power, a pattern dating back to colonial-era mobilization of jawara and vrijman through Soeharto's use of civilian militias.

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Muhammad Yoppy Adhi Hernawan argues that soldiers now teach Pancasila on campuses in an alarming sign of increasing militarization under President Prabowo Subianto. Military officers have delivered lectures at prestigious universities including Bandung Institute of Technology and Padjadjaran University, with one commander telling students to practice rather than debate Pancasila's meaning. Hernawan contends this represents a dangerous attempt to impose a single state interpretation of Indonesia's foundational ideology, undermining academic freedom and contradicting the principle that Pancasila's application is a matter for public argument, not military authority.

Writing in The Diplomat, Sebastian Strangio reports that President Prabowo has replaced his foreign minister with a career diplomat in a significant cabinet reshuffle that could reassert the Foreign Ministry's role in policymaking. Arrmanatha Nasir, a seasoned diplomat who joined the ministry in 1997 and served as ambassador to France and the UN, replaces political novice Sugiono, whose tenure was criticized for inconsistency and subordination to the presidential palace. The appointment marks a sharp contrast to Sugiono's lack of foreign affairs experience—his main credential being deputy chairperson of Prabowo's Gerindra Party—and may reduce the unpredictability that has characterized Indonesian foreign policy under the current administration. Writing in The Diplomat, Febry Triantama argues that with the arrival of the KRI Sriwijaya aircraft carrier, now comes the hard part of converting the vessel into a viable military capability. The former Italian carrier Giuseppe Garibaldi arrived in Jakarta in September following a controversial acquisition, but Triantama emphasizes that aircraft carriers never operate independently and require a carrier strike group including guided missile cruisers, anti-aircraft warships, anti-submarine destroyers, and supply ships. The key question is whether Indonesia can build and sustain the supporting ecosystem necessary for effective operations, as the ship alone does not equal strategic capability.
