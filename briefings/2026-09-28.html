What Happened Last Week

Vice President Gibran faced a Constitutional Court challenge over whether his educational credentials met the high school diploma requirement for presidential candidates.

The Constitutional Court began hearing a petition on September 21 from former deputy law minister Denny Indrayana and other petitioners arguing that Gibran did not meet the senior high school requirement and should be disqualified from his 2024 candidacy. Petitioners contended that a 2019 Education Ministry certificate stating Gibran's overseas studies were equivalent to Indonesian vocational high school failed to satisfy the law's stipulation. Judge Saldi Isra asked the KPU on September 21 for records explaining a 2023 rule change that exempted some overseas-educated candidates from submitting high school diplomas. Another judge questioned on September 25 why the KPU verified diplomas differently for local elections versus presidential elections. The case, docketed as 01/PHPU.PRES-XXIV/2026, challenges Gibran's candidacy two years after the 2024 election in which the Prabowo-Gibran ticket won with 58.6 percent of the vote.

Political parties debated a proposed rule requiring presidential candidates to be backed by at least two parliamentary factions ahead of the 2029 election.

NasDem Party chairman Surya Paloh welcomed the idea on September 19, saying he had heard of plans to require presidential tickets to have support from at least two factions in the DPR. Golkar's Ahmad Irawan explained on September 23 that under the proposal, one non-parliamentary party and one parliamentary party alone would not suffice, requiring an additional party already seated in the DPR. Analysts told The Straits Times on September 22 that the cost would fall hardest on younger aspirants without institutional backing, as seven of eight parliamentary parties belong to President Prabowo's coalition. Non-parliamentary parties rejected the proposal as contrary to the Constitutional Court's January 2025 decision abolishing the presidential threshold. The debate intensified as MPs prepared to amend the election law, with discussions also emerging about separating presidential and legislative elections.

Indonesia's delegation walked out of the UN General Assembly as Israeli Prime Minister Netanyahu began his address on September 24.

Indonesia's seven-member delegation rose and walked out as Netanyahu approached the podium, joining delegations from nearly 70 countries including Turkey, Malaysia, Iran, Saudi Arabia, and Egypt in a mass protest over Israel's actions in Gaza and the occupied territories. Netanyahu responded by calling the departing delegates "cowards" before delivering his 40-minute address to a largely empty chamber. Indonesia's Foreign Ministry spokesman Nabyl said the walkout reflected Indonesia's consistent stance supporting Palestinian independence and opposing violations of international law. Foreign Minister Sugiono declared at the UN that Palestine remains a test of the world's conscience, and Indonesia announced support for boycotting imports from illegal Israeli settlements. Indonesia also pledged $1.2 million to the UN agency for Palestinian refugees.

Expert Commentary This Week

Writing in The Diplomat, Juneman Abraham argues that Indonesia's rising public anger reflects a deeper psychological condition called ressentiment, a chronic internalized state of powerlessness and distrust that ressentiment persists even after protests fade. Abraham identifies five structural drivers reinforcing this sentiment, including visible elite impunity where corruption suspects smile at cameras and dynastic political maneuvers that bend legal norms for elite families. The widening gap between political families flaunting luxury goods and mothers fainting in rice queues creates a sense of moral injury, where Indonesians are angry not merely at corruption but at the shamelessness with which it is performed.

Writing at Indonesia at Melbourne, Feri Amsari argues that the secretive deliberation of Indonesia's Asset Forfeiture Bill raises concerns about elite capture, as no official draft has been released despite parliament's commitment to pass it by December 2026. Though the bill is ostensibly critical for fighting corruption and was championed by protesters from Pati demanding its passage, the lack of transparency mirrors recent problematic legislation including laws on the military, police, and job creation. Amsari warns this pattern of secretive lawmaking violates constitutional guarantees of public participation and suggests the final bill may serve political elite interests rather than anti-corruption objectives.
