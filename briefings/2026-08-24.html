What Happened Last Week

Tensions erupted in Aceh on August 15 when a man replaced the Indonesian flag with a GAM-linked crescent flag during a peace anniversary event, triggering clashes and warning shots.

The 21st commemoration of Aceh Peace Day at the Baiturrahman Grand Mosque turned violent when a young man climbed a flagpole and replaced the red-and-white with the crescent moon and star flag historically associated with the Free Aceh Movement. Military personnel ordered the flag removed before firing warning shots, sending crowds scrambling and prompting stone-throwing at security forces. The Jakarta Post reported that frustration over slow flood recovery likely fueled the unrest. By week's end, the TNI commander promoted two soldiers who removed the separatist flag, with Sergeant Irfan Farhan and Private Donni Pinto Harahap receiving extraordinary rank promotions for what authorities called courage and patriotism.

One Indonesian soldier died and two were wounded in a firefight with armed separatists in Papua's Mimika district during Independence Day celebrations.

Private Rizki Kurniawan was killed and two other soldiers injured when TPNPB-OPM forces attacked a TNI post in Jila district on August 17. Rizki, a member of Infantry Battalion 133/Yudha Sakti, suffered a fatal head wound while two colleagues, Privates Y Pasaribu and Kiki Febrio, were wounded. Days later, authorities confirmed that nine women and children were taken hostage by the separatist group as they retreated following the attack. Mimika Military District Commander Lt. Col. Jozanda said troops were deployed to pursue the perpetrators and rescue the hostages, who were reported missing only on Wednesday after soldiers conducted security sweeps in the remote highland area.

Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi and Defense Minister Dong Jun met President Prabowo on Friday following strategic talks focused on deepening bilateral cooperation.

The two Chinese ministers visited Prabowo at his Jakarta residence after attending the inaugural Comprehensive Strategic Dialogue and a second "2+2" meeting with their Indonesian counterparts. Prabowo praised Xi Jinping's leadership and called for strengthening cooperation in politics, economy, science and technology, while welcoming more Chinese investment to Indonesia. Both countries agreed to boost military ties and work closely on minerals, energy and technology, though Chinese companies have complained about stringent regulations and higher costs. Wang Yi said China places Indonesia in an important position in its diplomacy, while the talks also addressed finalizing a Code of Conduct for the South China Sea.

Expert Commentary This Week

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Aristyo Darmawan argues that Indonesia's joint naval passing exercise with China east of Taiwan risks putting Jakarta right in the middle of tensions between Beijing and Taipei despite its stated neutrality. While Indonesia insists it remains consistent with its One China Policy and does not recognize Taiwan's exclusive economic zone in the area, the exercise has provoked strong protests from Taipei and raised questions about whether Jakarta is maintaining its longstanding principle of not taking sides in regional conflicts. The controversy highlights the ambiguity in international law regarding military exercises in EEZs and the challenges Indonesia faces in balancing its free and active diplomacy with intensifying geopolitical pressures.

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Dio Ashar Wicaksana argues that the politically charged case of former Deputy Attorney General Febrie Adriansyah is just the latest chapter in the long-running story of rivalry between Indonesia's police and prosecutors. This rivalry traces back to the post-independence period when the two institutions disputed control over pre-trial investigations, with police rejecting their subordinate status and prosecutors insisting on supervisory authority to prevent abuse of coercive powers. President Prabowo's legally problematic decision to transfer Febrie's case from police to prosecutors, reportedly following intervention by military allies, highlights how this historical institutional conflict continues to shape Indonesia's criminal justice system today.

Writing in Fulcrum, Ary Hermawan and Norshahril Saat argue that President Prabowo's conspicuous absence from the Ulema Council of Indonesia's major congress signals that MUI is now just another player in a crowded field of Islamic organizations vying for presidential attention. Prabowo's failure to attend the Indonesian Muslim Community Congress in July, despite previous presidents' participation in such events, suggests MUI has fallen lower on his priority list, possibly due to the organization's criticism of his Gaza policy and his preference for engaging directly with larger groups like Nahdlatul Ulama. While this does not indicate a complete break, as MUI retains electoral weight, it reflects the president's strategy of diversifying Islamic partnerships rather than privileging any single supra-organization.

Writing in The Diplomat, Hugh Harsono argues that Japan is increasingly positioning itself as Indonesia's primary strategic partner, with Jakarta seeking partnerships that can strengthen its security and economy while respecting its non-aligned foreign policy. The two nations have substantially deepened cooperation in defense, including discussions on Japanese destroyer exports and submarine acquisitions, as well as energy security and regional training facilities. However, this growing relationship faces challenges from political uncertainty in both countries, economic headwinds in Indonesia, and public concerns that closer ties with Japan might compromise Indonesian neutrality given Tokyo's alignment with the United States.
