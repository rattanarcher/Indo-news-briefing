What Happened Last Week

Indonesia confirmed it is investigating reports that eight citizens were recruited into the Russian military to fight in Ukraine.

The controversy emerged after Ukraine's Foreign Intelligence Service published documents on August 27 identifying eight Indonesian men who had traveled to Russia on tourist visas between September 2025 and June 2026. Deputy Speaker Sufmi Dasco Ahmad revealed the men were allegedly recruited after initially applying for security guard or administrative positions, with intelligence authorities reporting they were eventually sent to serve as soldiers. Coordinating Minister Yusril Ihza Mahendra stated the government must verify identities, recruitment processes, and whether the individuals actually joined the Russian military before taking action. One of the eight was identified as a Depok resident who told his family he was traveling for tourism to Malaysia. Foreign Minister Sugiono said the eight Indonesians remain entitled to protection as long as they retain Indonesian citizenship, though the government can revoke citizenship if they joined a foreign military without presidential authorization.

President Prabowo Subianto attended the Eastern Economic Forum in Russia and proposed doubling bilateral trade through the Indonesia-EAEU free trade agreement.

Prabowo traveled to Vladivostok at Russian President Vladimir Putin's invitation to serve as guest of honor at the forum on September 3, marking his fourth visit to Russia and second visit in 2026. He called for doubling trade between Indonesia and Russia, noting that bilateral trade reached approximately $5 billion in 2025, a 21.7 percent increase from 2024. Prabowo emphasized that the Indonesia-Eurasian Economic Union Free Trade Agreement would open Indonesia's market of 290 million consumers to the EAEU's five member states while giving Indonesian exporters access to Eurasian markets. He described the pact as a new "South-South trade architecture" and urged member states to complete ratification procedures so the agreement could enter force in early 2027. Prabowo arrived back in Jakarta early Friday morning and later met with the U.S. ambassador at his Hambalang residence.

Democratic Party chairman Agus Harimurti Yudhoyono delivered pointed remarks at his party's anniversary, signaling criticism of Prabowo's government despite being part of the coalition.

Speaking at the Democratic Party's 25th anniversary celebration on September 5, AHY said the party must remain honest about conditions felt by the Indonesian people and should help improve policies that are not yet perfect. The coordinating minister for infrastructure acknowledged that people are still facing pressures from high prices and difficulty finding work, and he stressed that the military, police, intelligence services, and civil servants must remain neutral in every election. AHY also warned that power has limits and urged that people's right to vote should not be obstructed. Political analysts viewed the remarks as bold self-criticism and a reminder to his own boss amid declining approval ratings and economic concerns, with observers noting the move could upset Prabowo but also serve as political investment ahead of 2029.

Expert Commentary This Week

Writing in The Diplomat, Faqih Addien Al Haq and Liam Mullett argue that while Indonesia remains committed to its bebas aktif foreign policy doctrine, the material bases for a free and active foreign policy are eroding as China's economic influence rapidly deepens. They contend that Indonesia has moved furthest toward China among major ASEAN states since 1995, with Beijing now dominating critical sectors like electric vehicles, mineral processing, and telecommunications through what they term full-stack dependencies across entire value chains. This shift is especially consequential given Indonesia's strategic importance as ASEAN's only G20 member, raising the prospect that Jakarta may soon lack the economic autonomy to meaningfully resist Chinese preferences. The rapid replacement of agency heads following corruption allegations and operational failures reveals structural weaknesses in how the program was designed and rolled out, with personal connections outweighing expertise. Supriatma contends that while trusted appointees may impose discipline, political authority alone cannot transform institutions or ensure effective policy implementation at the massive scale Prabowo envisions.

Writing for Indonesia at Melbourne, Muhammad Beni Saputra argues that complicit local media have played a major role in legitimizing Indonesia's remilitarization under President Prabowo, as the military expands into civilian life through new laws and village-level programs. He contends that local media propagate positive narratives about TNI involvement due to their vulnerability to intimidation and lack of protective networks, unlike better-resourced national outlets owned by oligarchs with political connections. Saputra notes that local journalists face severe consequences for crossing lines, including job loss and even death, with nearly all journalists killed in peacetime Indonesia being local reporters.

Writing for Indonesia at Melbourne, Testriono argues that the Prabowo government's dramatic cuts to regional transfers—from 28 percent of total expenditure in 2023 to just 17 percent by 2026—represent fiscal recentralization that risks reversing important gains made through decentralisation since 2001. The 24.6 percent year-on-year reduction in transfer budgets is already affecting public services in 138 local governments, with some provinces warning they lack funds to pay civil servant salaries. Testriono suggests these cuts, partly driven by funding needs for programs like Free Nutritious Meals, undermine the core logic of bringing government closer to citizens and weaken local capacity to deliver public goods.

Writing for CSIS Indonesia, Pieter Pandie observes that the boundaries of what constitutes national defence in Indonesia have become increasingly difficult to define, with food security, energy, health, and even social welfare now discussed through security language. This conceptual expansion has accompanied the TNI's growing visibility in civilian programs ranging from agricultural development to the Free Nutritious Meals initiative and village cooperatives. Pandie's analysis suggests this blurring of civilian and military spheres reflects a fundamental shift in how the Indonesian state understands and organizes national security functions under the Prabowo administration.
