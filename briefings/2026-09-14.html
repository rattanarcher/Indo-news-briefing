What Happened Last Week

President Prabowo attended the 18th BRICS Summit in New Delhi, calling for Global South unity and emphasizing Indonesia's role in multilateral forums.

Prabowo arrived in India on Friday evening and was welcomed by Prime Minister Modi at the summit venue on Saturday. During the sessions, the president invoked the Indonesian proverb "Bersatu Kita Teguh, Terpecah Kita Runtuh" to underscore the importance of unity among developing nations. He also declared that Indonesia does not like being dictated to by certain powers. In his Sunday address, Prabowo urged BRICS countries to turn vulnerabilities into opportunities and proposed that members jointly pursue industrialization to optimize shared resources. The president held conversations with Modi, Putin, and Xi Jinping on the sidelines of the summit.

Partai Demokrat leader AHY's speech about limits to power sparked coalition tensions and interpretations that he was signaling a 2029 presidential run.

At Demokrat's 25th anniversary celebration on September 5, AHY emphasized that power has time limits and belongs to the people as an amanah. The remarks triggered reactions from pro-Prabowo parties, with Golkar's Ahmad Doli Kurnia viewing it as a signal AHY was preparing for the 2029 race. Demokrat officials quickly clarified that the speech was about democratic principles and reminding cadres that power is temporary. The controversy was defused when Prabowo attended the anniversary celebration on September 9 and praised Demokrat for not being destructive when out of power, while AHY reaffirmed the party would support government policies.

A proposal by village heads to extend or eliminate term limits drew pushback from DPR members and experts who warned it would undermine democracy.

The Association of Village Heads Ending Their Terms met with DPR Deputy Speaker Sufmi Dasco Ahmad on September 9, requesting that the 36,000 village heads facing term expiration through 2029 be allowed to continue serving, citing fiscal constraints and the need for stability. While Deputy Speaker Saan Mustopa acknowledged stability concerns, DPR Commission II member Muhammad Khozin warned the proposal could disrupt village-level democracy and saw no force majeure justifying elimination of term limits. Political observer M. Jamiluddin Ritonga rejected unlimited terms as only appropriate in authoritarian systems, while another DPR member warned that unlimited tenures could create "little kings" in villages.

Three civil servants from the Agriculture Office were shot dead in Jayawijaya, prompting a manhunt by security forces.

Three ASN were killed on September 9 in Muliama District while traveling on official duty, with victims identified as livestock chief Aprida Rapi, veterinarian Alfonsius Albinus Mehan, and civil service candidate Willi Mbuik. Police recovered three shell casings from the scene, including two 5.56mm rounds and one 9mm round. Cartenz Peace Operation Task Force spokesman Kombes Yusuf Sutejo said police had obtained descriptions of the perpetrators and launched a pursuit. Authorities identified two suspects also linked to an August shooting at the Baliem Valley Festival. The Ministry of Human Rights condemned the killings, rejecting claims by armed groups that the victims were intelligence agents.

Expert Commentary This Week

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Ary Hermawan argues that Nahdlatul Ulama's recent leadership election represents a deepening state co-option of the Islamic group under President Prabowo Subianto. The new leaders, widely seen as the palace package favoured by Prabowo's government, signal a troubling departure from NU's historic role under figures like Abdurrahman Wahid, who maintained the organisation's independence from the state. Hermawan contends that greater grassroots engagement is now needed to pressure NU elites to maintain the organisation's past role as a defender of democratic norms and promoter of civil Islam, reversing a decade-long trend of forging stronger alliances with ruling elites.

Writing in Fulcrum, Muyi Yang, Dody Setiawan, and Dinita Setyawati argue that Indonesia faces a strategic choice between redistribution or upgrading in its nickel sector management under President Prabowo's resource nationalism. While the government's instinct to capture a bigger slice of resource revenues through centralised oversight and stricter export controls is understandable, the authors contend that upgrading the sector to strengthen industrial capabilities would be a more positive-sum strategy. They argue that using Indonesia's nickel base to cultivate broader industrial capabilities would help grow the economy rather than simply redistributing existing rents, though the former pathway offers more immediate fiscal appeal for a government under pressure.
