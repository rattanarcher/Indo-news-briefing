What Happened Last Week

Vice President Gibran Rakabuming Raka resigned on November 12 amid a dispute over his high school diploma, triggering a search for his successor.

Gibran submitted his resignation to President Prabowo and parliament on Thursday after the Constitutional Court confirmed he did not have proof of high school graduation. MPR Speaker Ahmad Muzani received the letter, which contained no stated reasons for the departure. The resignation ended days of speculation about his political future and marked a symbolic end to the alliance between Prabowo and predecessor Joko Widodo. Prabowo said he will defer naming vice presidential candidates until after completing visits to the ASEAN Summit in the Philippines and APEC Summit in China, saying he needs divine guidance to find the most suitable successor. Eight political figures have emerged as potential candidates, including Puan Maharani and Bahlil Lahadalia, though Prabowo has 14 days to submit two names to the MPR for selection.

President Prabowo attended the 49th ASEAN Summit in Manila and apologized to Singapore and Malaysia for transboundary haze from Indonesian forest fires.

Prabowo arrived at the Philippine International Convention Center on Sunday morning for the summit running November 15-17. Speaking at the plenary session, Prabowo apologized for the impact of forest and land fires that have burned 2.88 million hectares this year and affected neighboring countries. While insisting the transboundary haze was "not our fault" and that Indonesia had done its best, he pledged continued firm action against companies linked to the blazes. Prabowo reaffirmed Indonesia's commitment to preventing forest fires and said his administration would not tolerate corporate involvement in the recurring environmental crisis. The haze issue drew significant attention during summit preparations, with Malaysia and the Philippines also raising concerns about the transboundary pollution.

A wiretapping scandal emerged after police detained three senior generals suspected of illegally surveilling President Prabowo and dozens of other prominent figures.

Indonesian police announced on November 3 they had detained three senior police generals for allegedly wiretapping President Prabowo and other prominent figures without legal authorization. Police Chief Gen. Suyudi Ario Seto told lawmakers on November 12 that the illegal operation extended beyond the president to senior government officials, politicians, activists, and law enforcement personnel. Speaking at the Muhammadiyah Tanwir on November 14, Prabowo praised the newly appointed Suyudi, saying he had initially been unfamiliar with him but that appointing him as police chief was the right decision that helped save the country. The Criminal Investigation Agency named three suspects on November 3 in connection with wiretapping without legal procedures, illegal access, and deletion of electronic data, allegedly targeting multiple parties including the president and state officials.

Expert Commentary This Week

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Yance Arizona argues that President Prabowo's bloated cabinet of 106 ministers and officials represents a purely political bargain to secure legislative support rather than any defensible governance need. By forming a governing coalition controlling 81 percent of parliament, Prabowo's seemingly boundless politics of accommodation has crippled the legislature's oversight function, leaving it unable to check executive power. This dysfunctional arrangement has enabled policy chaos including eight cabinet reshuffles in two years and legislation that expands executive authority while restricting accountability, prompting civil society organizations to form their own shadow cabinet.

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Ary Hermawan argues that Vice President Gibran's potential resignation stems less from the Supreme Court invalidating his educational credentials than from a deeper political purge targeting former President Jokowi's political network. The arrests of senior police officials including former chief Listyo Sigit Prabowo on wiretapping allegations represent a major escalation in the longstanding power struggle between Prabowo and Jokowi, whose support was instrumental in Prabowo's 2024 victory. This political quagmire threatens the so-called Solo faction comprising Jokowi's family and allies, which analysts view as a serious threat to Prabowo ahead of the 2029 election.

Writing in Indonesia at Melbourne, Alvino Kusumabrata argues that the Constitutional Court's decision on Vice President Gibran's educational credentials demonstrates limited judicial activism that stopped short of its full potential. While the court found there is no convincing evidence that Gibran possessed documents proving he completed education equivalent to senior high school, it dismissed the petition on narrow standing grounds despite identifying special circumstances that allowed it to examine the case. The court should have developed a broader interpretation recognizing that electoral disputes concern citizens harmed by procedural violations, not just candidates, thereby treating voting rights substantively rather than procedurally.

Writing in The Diplomat, Eka Nugraha Putra argues that Indonesia's August 2026 arrests of two citizens over online posts responding to President Prabowo's false claim about Iran's nuclear weapons demonstrate concerning reliance on criminal sanctions to address dissent. Authorities applied problematic provisions from the ITE Law and Criminal Code inconsistently with their intended purposes, raising concerns that criminal legislation may be used as a tool to silence dissenting views rather than address genuine threats or incitement. The case highlights failures to distinguish protected political satire and legitimate criticism from unlawful expression, particularly when arising from controversial government statements. [source]
